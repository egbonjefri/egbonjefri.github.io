Jeffery Osagie

I'm a physiologist and software developer in Winnipeg. I earned my bachelor's in Physiology at the University of Benin, then an MSc in Physiology & Pathophysiology at the University of Manitoba, where I worked on a 3D-bioprinted airway smooth muscle model to study airway mechanics in asthma.

During my master's, I photographed bioprinted airways before and after adding contractile agents, then traced the opening of each ring by hand to measure how much it narrowed. The tracing was tedious and easy to get wrong. Looking for a way to automate it got me into programming. I was hooked the first time I opened a terminal and kept learning, eventually moving into full-stack development.

The original idea turned into BioRing Analyzer, which uses TensorFlow.js and OpenCV.js to detect bioprinted airway rings and measure changes in lumen area in the browser. I've since started modelling airway narrowing with a mass-spring simulation of the airway wall that shows how tissue stiffness limits the narrowing caused by smooth muscle contraction. I've also built a bio-ink calculator and a stroke prediction app. The stroke app was a coursework project, with a backend in R using Plumber.

Learning how computers work has given me another way to think about physiology. Just like computer processors, cells in our bodies process information too. They do this through networks of interacting proteins that can behave much like logic circuits. An airway smooth muscle cell, for example, combines signals that promote contraction or relaxation to adjust how much force it generates. I'm curious about how we can engineer these networks to carry out tasks we choose, such as detecting signs of disease and triggering a therapeutic response.

I started with TOY-8, a small 8-bit teaching processor, working out how it could be built from NOR gates alone and how many gates it would take. I want to understand how individual gates form a working computer and what would need to change to build similar circuits from proteins.

Both the circuits and the airway models are still a long way from the clinic, and I'm planning to go to medical school to close that gap. I'd like to learn how disease behaves in real patients and build models that help the people I end up treating.

Studying for the MCAT has led to another project. I kept wanting to work out why things were true, but the prep apps I tried mostly asked me to memorize them. So I'm building MCAT Aletheia, where students work through derivations and experiment with interactive models until they can see why each result has to be true.

I love logic and philosophy. Sometimes I'm debating Parmenides versus Kant with Claude at 3 in the morning, trying to pin down what we mean by “change”. I also play chess, and I'm a sucker for puzzle games like Portal and The Talos Principle.
